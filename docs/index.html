Who is in the Image?
Benchmarking Any Identity Anonymization
in Multimodal Large Language Models

Simone Caldarella1 Matteo Farina1 Massimiliano Mancini1 Elisa Ricci1,2 Rahaf Aljundi3
1University of Trento 2Fondazione Bruno Kessler 3Toyota Motor Europe
Paper Code Dataset BibTeX

Can a multimodal model describe a person without revealing who they are? We introduce TUTUS to study identity anonymization without a predefined forget set, jointly measuring privacy, descriptive utility, and resistance to malicious prompts.

Abstract

We study the problem of preventing identity leakage of any individual in multimodal large language models (MLLMs). To enable standardized evaluation, we introduce TUTUS, a multimodal benchmark that assesses anonymization along three complementary desiderata: identity suppression, utility preservation, and robustness to malicious inputs. Using TUTUS, we systematically evaluate training-free and post-training alignment strategies across six MLLMs. Popular training-free methods, including face blurring and prompt engineering, are brittle despite appearing effective under isolated metrics. We investigate post-training using a preference dataset to align models toward identity anonymization. Our analyses show that the trade-off depends on the alignment objective and model component, with vision-encoder tuning offering favorable trade-offs in the evaluated LLaVA and Qwen settings. We also evaluate transfer to unseen celebrity identities and to synthetic identities introduced through downstream adaptation.

A benchmark for identity privacy

Selective unlearning starts with a known set of identities to forget. Any identity anonymization instead asks a model to suppress name disclosure without that predefined set, while keeping non-identifying visual information useful. TUTUS evaluates this objective using celebrity and synthetic identities.

Scope. “Any identity” names the objective. Celebrity and synthetic evaluation does not establish universal protection for arbitrary real-world individuals.

Privacy, utility, and robustness together

  • Relative Suppression Success · RSS. How often does anonymization suppress a name that the base model disclosed?
  • Contextual Description Preservation · CDP. How similar are the model’s image descriptions before and after anonymization?
  • Jailbreak Defense Ratio · JDR. How much of that suppression remains under malicious prompt variations?

One metric alone can hide a failure. Suppressing names by destroying visual information sacrifices utility; strong direct suppression can still collapse under a simple name-only request. TUTUS makes these trade-offs visible.

Where should privacy alignment happen?

  • Training-free safeguards remain brittle. Prompting, blurring, noise injection, and visual-token pruning expose different privacy–utility trade-offs.
  • The alignment location matters. We compare the vision encoder, connector, and language model under SFT, gradient ascent, DPO, and NPO.
  • Vision-encoder alignment is a useful empirical choice. It often provides favorable trade-offs in the evaluated LLaVA and Qwen models.
  • Transfer is part of the evaluation. Anonymization is tested on unseen celebrity identities and after personalization on fictitious individuals.

Preferences for identity anonymization

The training resource pairs an image and identity query with a preferred non-identifying response and a rejected leaking response. This lets researchers compare alignment objectives while retaining the same privacy task.

The code release includes the experiment settings, dataset loaders, training-free baselines, post-training objectives, adaptation evaluation, attack and refusal analysis, and five utility benchmarks.
